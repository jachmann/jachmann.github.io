Research

Understanding language in context

Comprehension is not a passive decoding of words. Listeners and readers continuously predict what comes next, drawing on the visual scene, the speaker, the discourse and the grammar of their language. My work asks which cues feed these expectations, when they are used, and what happens when they are confirmed or violated.

01

Speaker gaze and referential expectations

In face-to-face conversation, speakers tend to look at an object shortly before they name it. Listeners can exploit this: a glance towards the house makes house the expected next word. In a series of EEG experiments, a virtual speaker looked at one of several objects while a sentence unfolded; the gaze either matched the noun that followed, contradicted it, or was absent.

Event-related potentials show that listeners use the gaze cue immediately: incongruent gaze changes the brain’s response to the noun within a few hundred milliseconds, and later components suggest that listeners update their model of the situation. The 2023 Cognition article shows that when gaze points to an unexpected object, the brain responds to the gaze cue itself (N400, P300) and the effects on the following noun are attenuated: listeners treat speaker gaze as an integral part of the communicative signal.

02

Word order, givenness and the expected-first principle

Languages with flexible word order make object-first sentences harder to process, and readers prefer given information before new. Expectation-based accounts explain both: canonical orders and given-before-new are simply more predictable. My current work, part of SFB 1102 project C3, tests a stronger claim – that comprehenders rely heavily on expectations about the first noun phrase, and that costs arise whenever it diverges from them.

Self-paced reading studies in German (HSP 2024) manipulated word order and three grades of givenness – given, implied (inferable from context) and new. A parallel study in Korean, a language with productive topic and subject drop, shows that the same expectation system surfaces differently depending on case marking and argument drop.

03

Collaborations and earlier work

With colleagues I have worked on how the question under discussion shapes discourse expectations (ERPs), on age-related differences in learning new words from expectations, and on how informativity affects the order in which speakers mention referents.

My MSc thesis asked whether prosody works as a turn-taking cue across German, English and Japanese, building on joint work on prosody and gaze in turn-end anticipation. My BSc thesis and a paper at NLP2014 dealt with pre-editing rules for machine-translating English forum posts into Japanese.
